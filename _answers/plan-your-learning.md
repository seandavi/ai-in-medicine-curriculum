# Answer key: Plan your four weeks with ChatGPT

For the tutor and instructors only. Never rendered.

There is no single right plan. The plan is the student's own, so the key
describes what a good one looks like and how to respond to a weak one.

## How the session runs

The Thursday session is 60 minutes, including the director's opening. In the
session: set up, then step 1 (research and open two links), step 3 (the
interview and goals), and step 4 (the plan, with the course list inside the
prompt). Step 2 (themes) is optional. After the session, before that night's
journal: step 5 (the journal template) and step 6 (sharing, optional). Step 7
is the weekly revisit.

## What a good plan shows

Use the Check table in the chapter. In practice, look for:

- **Research it can name.** The student can say which source, or theme if
  they did step 2, led to each part of the plan. Typical themes: how these
  systems work and fail; checking outputs and evidence; bias and equity;
  privacy and ethics; talking with patients; using AI in daily work; how AI
  changes the specialty. A plan that lists only course chapters, with no
  source behind them, skipped the research.
- **The student in it.** A specialty, a learning style, a worry, or a goal no
  source suggested ("I want to stop being scared of it" is a real goal).
- **A check done.** The student opened two sources and can say whether each
  said what ChatGPT reported. Most will. A source that did not exist, or did
  not say what was claimed, is a good result to discuss, not a failure.
- **A topic to learn with AI each week.** Learn it with AI
  (`course/learn-with-ai.qmd`, 2 to 4 hours a week) is the main hands-on work
  of the course, and journal question 1 asks how the week's topic connects to
  the week 1 goals. The step 4 prompt also asks which goals the course does
  not cover; those are natural topics.
- **A plan that fits.** About 8 to 10 hours a week. Twenty chapters and five
  projects is not a plan.
- **A way to change it.** The student knows they will revisit it each week.

## Common problems and one question for each

Ask one question; do not rewrite the plan.

| Problem | Ask |
|---|---|
| Generic plan, could be anyone's | "What is your specialty, and what about AI worries you most?" |
| No research behind it | "Which source led you to pick this chapter?" |
| Did not open any sources | "Open two of the links now. Do they say what ChatGPT said?" |
| No topic to learn with AI | "What is one thing you want to learn with AI this week, and why that?" |
| Too much | "If you could do only one thing in four weeks, what would it be?" |
| Too vague ("learn about AI") | "What would you be able to do in week 4 that you cannot do now?" |
| Plan is ChatGPT's wording throughout | "Which of these goals would you change if a colleague read them?" |

## Things that go wrong in the session

- **ChatGPT works from the pasted course list,** not the book's website,
  which it cannot read. The list is inside the step 4 prompt. If a student
  cut it, the course parts in the plan will be invented; have them paste the
  whole prompt and ask again.
- **No links in the research answer.** Ask ChatGPT to search the web. If the
  account has search turned off, the student can still do steps 3 to 7 and
  name the missing research in the journal.
- **Personal account instead of the university one.** Check the bottom left
  of the screen. Fix it at office hours; the student can move the chat later
  by pasting the plan into a new chat.
- **ChatGPT Edu account not ready.** Use the university Microsoft Copilot
  account for the session and move the plan over later. Not a free or
  personal chatbot: the course rule is the university account.
- **No Projects in the account.** Start an ordinary chat named AI in
  Medicine. The plan document carries what matters from week to week.
- **On a phone.** The prompts are long and the links open side by side;
  a laptop works much better.
- **The file download does not work.** Copy the table into Word or Google
  Docs.

## Follow-up

The chapter's closing section points the student to two journal questions:
question 1 (what I chose to learn, and why) with one goal they kept that no
source told them to, and question 3 (what surprised me) with what the
research showed them that they would not have picked on their own. Question
2's check can be the two sources opened in step 1. Students who revisit the
plan will say so in question 1 in weeks 2 and 3. A changed plan is the result
the chapter is after. The week 4 "what you will keep" piece is a natural
place to compare the week 1 plan with what happened.
