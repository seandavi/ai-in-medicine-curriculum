## Part A — model answers

Model answers, not the only answers. The tutor grades the shape (three
sentences, in order, separate facts, a name in the third) and the two extras,
not the wording. The student writes these before asking the chatbot anything.

**1. The phone on the desk.**

> It's listening to us and writing a first draft of my note. I read the draft,
> change it, and sign it; the note is mine, not the phone's. If you'd rather it
> didn't, I'll turn it off now and type the note myself; the visit is the same
> either way. I don't know how long the recording is kept or who at the company
> can access it, and I'm not going to guess; I'll find out and message you
> through the portal this week.

Common failures: one sentence covering what it did and what the clinician does
("it helps me with the note"); a made-up retention figure ("it's deleted after
24 hours"); "nothing leaves the building" without knowing; no offer to turn it
off; the offer framed as a burden ("but then I'd have to type everything").

**2. The computer found something.** The sentence *before* the spine responds
to the fear: "You've been frightened for two days, and I'm sorry the phone call
left you with a word and no explanation. Let me tell you exactly what happened,
and then I'll answer the question you're going to ask." Then:

> The software marked one area on your mammogram for a closer look. Dr. Chen,
> the radiologist, read the whole study herself and agreed that area needs
> another picture. The decision to bring you back is hers and mine, not the
> software's.

Then the cancer question, which will come regardless: "Most areas flagged this
way turn out not to be cancer. I can't tell you it isn't until we have the
next pictures, and I won't tell you it is, because nobody knows yet." Watch for
the student who leads with how the software works, and for the one who says "it's
probably nothing."

**3. The alert you cannot explain.**

> I don't know how that alert decides when to fire, and I'm not going to guess.
> I'll ask the team that runs it and tell you what I learn at your next visit,
> or by message before then.

Both sentences fail if either reassures ("but it's very accurate"). The second
must carry a time.

**The chatbot review.** The prompt asks the tool to flag words, flag claims the
student could not know, and predict the next question, and not to rewrite. What
to look for: the student changed jargon the tool flagged ("ambient",
"computer-aided detection", "false positive") to plain words, removed any
sentence that claims knowledge they do not have (a retention period, "nothing
leaves the building"), and kept their own voice. A student who pasted a full
rewrite from the chatbot has missed the point; ask which sentence they would
actually say. If the tool rewrote them anyway despite the instruction, that is
a true observation about the tool, and the student should say what they did
next.

## Part B — what a good transcript looks like

Order of events in a strong run:

1. Clinician acknowledges the pages and names what is right (ferritin, TSH,
   sleep, mood) within the first two exchanges.
2. Clinician asks what prompted the search, or what happened last time, by
   exchange four; the model reveals the eight-minute visit and "probably
   stress."
3. Clinician responds to that ("that would send me looking too") before
   returning to the list.
4. Each declined test gets a reason. Reference reasons: cortisol (no
   orthostasis, hyperpigmentation, weight loss, or electrolyte change; a random
   morning cortisol is hard to interpret and often leads to more testing); ANA
   (no joint, skin, or systemic features; low pre-test probability makes a
   positive more likely false than true); Lyme (no exposure, rash, or
   arthritis; serology in low-prevalence settings is mostly false positives);
   EBV panel (reactivation serology does not explain fatigue and is not
   actionable); heavy metals (no exposure history); sleep study (reasonable
   *after* a sleep history, not before; agree conditionally). Vitamin D: a
   defensible either way; the tutor accepts a reason in either direction.
5. Iron: hold until ferritin is back; if normal, iron does nothing but cause GI
   effects; if high, that is itself a finding. A student who agrees to the iron
   "since it's low-risk" has given in.
6. One concrete next step: the two tests today, a sleep history now, a return
   visit or portal message with results by a date.

Failure patterns the tutor should name: winning the argument (all six tests
declined correctly, hidden detail never surfaced); giving in (ordering the
panel to end the conversation); talking down ("the internet isn't a doctor");
jargon (serology, pre-test probability, false positive) with no plain word.

The model's observer score is a language model's reading of a transcript. The
tutor should treat it as a prompt for the student's own reflection, not a
grade; the chapter says so.

## The empathy finding (objective 4), in a sentence the tutor can accept

Raters preferred the chatbot's text and called it more empathetic (Ayers 2023,
pmid:37115527; replicated with mechanism in Ruben 2025, pmid:41359230), but the
same kind of text produced the smallest change in what people expected from a
treatment (Thomae 2024, pmid:39177012). Limits the student should name: Ayers
is text judged by readers, no relationship, no outcomes, unlimited-time tool
versus rushed physicians; Thomae confounds author with medium (humans on video,
chatbot as synthetic voice-over) and measures students' own expectations, not
patients'.

## Instructor prep

The chapter's spoken practice now happens in the week 3 Friday small groups
(October 30), using the cards in `course/friday-groups.qmd`: the scribe
question and the caregiver who came prepared rehearse Parts A and B. Run the
Part B prompt yourself before week 3 in ChatGPT Edu and Copilot. If the persona
caves on the first exchange, the chapter already tells students to add "be
harder to persuade"; if it still caves, add that line to the prompt itself.
The same Part B prompt is the "patient who talks back" open lab; if the class
votes for it, run it as written, then have students change the patient.
