# Enrichment module: Writing with AI and keeping your voice

**Format:** self-paced chapter, about 75 minutes, paired with a talk on
professional writing with AI · **Prereq:** none
**Competencies:** AIM-4 (ethics, law, and regulation: attribution and
academic integrity), AIM-5 (clinical application and practical use), AIM-X
(professional identity)

## What this module is for

Students already use chatbots to write: application entries, emails,
abstracts. The common worry is detection ("will they know?"). The evidence
says that worry is misplaced: faculty readers identify AI-written personal
statements at about chance, and sometimes score them higher. So this module
moves the question from *getting caught* to three things the evidence does
support:

1. **Sameness.** Model-written text converges across writers; writer-written
   text does not (Padmakumar & He, ICLR 2024; Doshi & Hauser, *Sci Adv*
   2024; Traylor, *JGME* 2026; Agarwal, CHI 2025).
2. **Ownership.** Copying AI output lowers ownership and self-efficacy;
   drafting first and then refining with AI is close to working alone (Lee,
   *Sci Rep* 2026; Gero, DIS 2026).
3. **Views.** An opinionated assistant shifts what writers say they believe
   (Jakesch, CHI 2023).

It is deliberately narrow: voice, ownership, and honesty in AI-assisted
writing. It is not a module on professional writing in general; the paired
talk covers that ground.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student can:

1. Explain why "readers can't tell" does not settle whether to let AI draft,
   naming sameness, ownership, or views the writer does not hold.
2. Use a chatbot as interviewer and critic on a real piece of their own
   writing, with the core drafted by the student.
3. Check a revised piece for overused chatbot vocabulary and for voice drift.
4. State what the residency application rules allow and write a one-line
   disclosure.
5. Explain why AI-detector scores are not evidence of misconduct, and why
   "humanizer" tools do not address the actual problem.

## The Do (35 to 40 minutes, one student, browser only)

1. Pick a real, non-patient piece of writing; write a three-line "done"
   rubric (reader, the one thing to know, what I would say if asked).
2. The chatbot interviews the student (five questions, writes nothing).
3. The student drafts the core alone, 100 to 250 words, first person.
4. The chatbot critiques the draft against the rubric; no rewrite. The
   student revises.
5. Two checks: a marker-word search, and a fresh-chat "same author?"
   comparison against an older sample of the student's writing. The second
   is a heuristic with no evidence behind it, and is labelled so.
6. A one-line disclosure.

Optional: a minimal-edit prompt seeded with the student's past writing
(Wang et al., Findings of EMNLP 2025: few-shot style imitation helps only
partly).

## Design notes

- **Success first.** The first AI step (being interviewed) is one the tool
  does well and that the evidence treats as low-cost to ownership (Gero:
  planning support lowered ownership least).
- **No patient information.** Personal statements often contain patient
  stories. The Do tells students to replace any such passage with a
  placeholder before pasting.
- **Fairness.** Detectors flag non-native and plain English at high rates
  (Liang, *Patterns* 2023; Subillaga, *J Surg Educ* 2025). The module says
  so, and tells students not to judge their own or others' writing by a
  detector score.
- **Frame.** "The goal is to sound like you, not to pass as you." Humanizer
  and paraphrasing tools are named and discouraged on honesty grounds, not
  on detection grounds.
- **Preprint.** Kosmyna et al. (arXiv 2025, "Your Brain on ChatGPT") is
  cited only in Go deeper and labelled as a preprint with contested methods.
- **Swap for another institution.** The AAMC quote is US-specific and dated;
  replace it with the local application body's current guidance.
