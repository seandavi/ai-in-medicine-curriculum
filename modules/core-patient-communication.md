# Core module — Talking to patients about AI

**Format:** one 90-minute Zoom session · **Prereq:** none
**Competencies:** AIM-6 (communication with patients about AI), AIM-4 (ethics, law, regulation, professionalism)

## Why this is the signature session

Communication with patients about AI is named in nearly every published
competency framework — Russell, Singla, Hunt, and the AAMC's stated goals — and
taught in only 21% of existing programs. Nothing else in this course sits on a
gap that wide between what the field says matters and what the field actually
teaches. It is also the one competency here that cannot be met by a lecture,
because the skill is not knowing what to say; it is saying it to a person who is
frightened, or skeptical, or already convinced the chatbot was right.

The design consequence is that this session is almost entirely role-play. It
runs on the infrastructure the school already has for OSCEs — scenario cards,
rotating roles, an observer with a rubric — which is the cheapest high-impact
move available and the one the competency literature explicitly recommends. What
the students take away is not a script. It is having already had the
conversation once, badly, in a room where that was free.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student can:

1. Explain to a patient, in plain language and under a minute, what an AI tool
   contributed to their care and what a clinician contributed.
2. State who is accountable for a clinical decision that an AI tool informed,
   and say it out loud without hedging.
3. Conduct a disclosure conversation about an AI tool in the encounter — ambient
   documentation, decision support, an assisted image read — including what the
   patient can decline and what they cannot.
4. Respond to a patient who arrives with chatbot output that is confident,
   articulate, and partly correct, without either capitulating or condescending.
5. Explain why AI-generated patient-facing material, even when accurate, tends
   to move patients less than the same information from a person, and why that
   makes clinician mediation part of the job rather than a courtesy.
6. Recognize when they do not know enough about a tool used in their patient's
   care to answer the patient's question, and say that in a way that keeps trust
   intact.

## The evidence students should have in hand

Two findings do most of the work in this session, and they point in opposite
directions, which is the point.

**Ayers et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2023.** Blinded evaluators compared
chatbot and physician responses to patient questions and preferred the chatbot
responses 78.6% of the time, rating them roughly ten times more empathetic. The
chatbot responses were also about four times longer. Students almost always
assume the failure mode of AI in patient communication is coldness. It is not.
The machine can outperform a rushed physician on the dimension physicians assume
is theirs.

**Thomae, Witt & Barth, *JMIR Medical Education*, 2024 (University of Zurich).**
Medical students critically reviewed patient-facing information written by
ChatGPT under different prompts and compared it for persuasiveness. Output from
a basic prompt rated only moderate on comprehensibility, patient safety, and
correct application of taught communication rules; an extended prompt improved
those ratings substantially. But the AI-written text still produced the
*smallest* increase in treatment expectations compared with the same information
delivered by a patient, a clinician, or an expert.

Put together: text rated as more empathetic and more preferred still moves
patients less than a person does. Empathy on the page is not the same thing as
influence in the room, and the gap between them is where the clinician's job
lives. That is the intellectual spine of the session — say it in the frame, and
come back to it in the close.

## Before class

No accounts, no installs, no tools. This is the one session in the course that
needs nothing but people. What it does need:

- **Scenario cards distributed 24 hours ahead**, one set per breakout room, so
  the patient role has time to read. Do not hand them out live; reading a card
  cold eats four minutes of every rotation.
- **The observer rubric posted in Zoom chat and pinned** at the start, and
  linked in the pre-work note so observers have seen it before their first turn.
- **A named convention that breakouts are not recorded.** Students perform badly
  in role-play when they think a recording exists, and this session is worthless
  if they perform safely.

Every scenario is fiction written for this course. No real patient, no real
encounter, no PHI in any room at any point — including the improvised detail
students invent while playing the patient, which should stay invented.

## Session plan (90 min)

| Time | Mode | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10 | Frame | The 21% gap: this is the thing everyone says matters and almost nobody teaches. The three standing rules, plus the session's own rule: no recording, all patients fictional. |
| 10–25 | Didactic + poll | The two findings above, and the disclosure spine below. Poll the room first — *when AI helps read your patient's scan, does the patient need to be told?* — then show that the room disagrees, which is the honest state of the field. |
| 25–70 | Breakout triads | Three rotations. Each student plays clinician once, patient once, observer once. Six minutes of encounter, five minutes of rubric-led debrief, two minutes to swap. |
| 70–82 | Report-back | Rooms that ran different scenarios compare. Seed it: *which room had the conversation that went worst, and what specifically broke?* |
| 82–90 | Close | The standing question, sharpened: what will you say to the first patient who asks you whether a computer was involved in their care? |

**Deliverable:** three completed observer rubrics per triad, one per student, kept
by the student they describe. Ungraded, unsubmitted, and useless to anyone else —
which is exactly why students will write honestly on them.

## Running the triads

Forty students makes thirteen triads and one group of four; the group of four
runs two observers, which works fine and produces the most useful debrief in the
class. Assign each room three of the six scenario cards, and vary the assignment
across rooms so report-back has something to compare. Rooms that all ran the same
card produce twelve versions of one conversation and nothing to argue about.

Roles rotate every round: the clinician becomes the observer, the observer
becomes the patient, the patient becomes the clinician. Playing the patient is
not the throwaway role — it is where students discover that the thing they say as
a clinician lands nothing like the way they meant it.

Faculty or near-peer facilitators should drop into rooms without joining the
scene. The failure mode of remote role-play is that students narrate the
conversation instead of having it ("so I'd probably explain that the AI..."). The
only fix is a facilitator saying, once, "say it to them."

## Scenario cards

Each card gives the clinician a situation and the patient a position, motivation,
and one thing they are not going to volunteer. The hidden detail is what makes
the encounter a conversation instead of a recitation.

### Card 1 — The scribe on the desk

**Clinician.** Routine follow-up, primary care. Your clinic went live last month
with an ambient documentation tool that listens to the visit and drafts the note.
You are expected to use it. Four minutes into the visit the patient points at the
phone on the desk and asks what it is doing.

**Patient.** 58, here about blood pressure. You work in HR and you have sat
through enough recorded-conversation policies to be alert to this. You are not
hostile, you are procedural: you want to know who hears it, how long it is kept,
and whether it goes anywhere outside this building. **You will not volunteer**
that your real worry is your employer, because your health plan runs through
work.

**What is being tested.** Whether the clinician can describe what the tool does
without jargon, admit the limits of what they personally know about the vendor
contract, and say plainly what the patient can decline. Watch for the student who
invents a reassuring answer about data retention. That is the failure.

### Card 2 — The computer found something

**Clinician.** You are covering breast clinic. A screening mammogram was read
with FDA-cleared computer-aided detection; the software flagged a region, the
radiologist agreed, and diagnostic views are recommended. The patient was called
by a scheduler who said "the computer found something on your scan."

**Patient.** 47, no family history, terrified since the phone call two days ago.
You have not slept. You want to know whether a real doctor looked at it, whether
the computer can be wrong, and — the question you will circle back to no matter
what is said — whether this means you have cancer. **You will not volunteer** that
your sister had a false positive that led to a biopsy and a year of anxiety, and
that this is the picture in your head.

**What is being tested.** Emotion before facts. The accurate technical
explanation — software flags, human reads, human decides — is worthless if it
arrives before the fear is acknowledged. Also watch whether the clinician
correctly separates "the AI flagged it" from "the radiologist agreed," because
those are different facts and patients conflate them.

### Card 3 — But ChatGPT said

**Clinician.** New patient, six weeks of fatigue. Labs and exam so far are
unremarkable. The patient arrives with two printed pages from a chatbot: a
differential, a list of tests, and a suggested plan. Roughly a third of it is
reasonable, a third is over-testing, and one item is actively unhelpful.

**Patient.** 34, software developer, articulate and not remotely embarrassed
about this. You are not attacking the clinician; you genuinely think you have
done useful homework and you have. You want the tests. **You will not volunteer**
that what actually drove you to the chatbot was that the last physician you saw
gave you eight minutes and told you it was probably stress.

**What is being tested.** Whether the student can say "this part is right" before
saying "this part is wrong," and whether they can find the reason the patient
went to the chatbot at all. Any clinician who wins the argument and loses the
patient has failed this card. The observer should specifically note whether the
clinician ever asked what prompted the search.

### Card 4 — The handout that did nothing

**Clinician.** Post-discharge phone follow-up. The patient was sent home four
days ago with discharge instructions your service drafts with an AI tool and a
clinician reviews before it goes out. The patient has not been taking one of the
new medications correctly.

**Patient.** 71, discharged after a heart failure admission. You read the
instructions. They were clear enough, but they read like a pamphlet and nothing
in them felt like it was about you, so you followed what your neighbor said
instead. **You will not volunteer** that you did not believe the instructions
mattered much, because nobody had told you they mattered.

**What is being tested.** This is the Thomae finding as an encounter. Clear,
accurate, comprehensible text produced the smallest change in what the patient
expected and did. The clinician's job is not to fix the handout; it is to supply
the thing the handout structurally cannot, which is a person saying *this one
matters, here is why, tell me back what you are going to do.*

### Card 5 — I'd rather you didn't

**Clinician.** Established patient, annual visit. Before you begin, the patient
tells you they do not want any AI involved in their care — not the scribe, not
whatever is flagging things in the chart, none of it.

**Patient.** 62, has read enough news to be genuinely worried, and is not going to
be talked out of it in one visit. You want a straight answer about what you can
actually refuse. **You will not volunteer** that you would accept most of it if
someone explained who is responsible when the software is wrong; that is the
answer you are actually looking for.

**What is being tested.** Honesty about the limits of the clinician's control.
Some things are genuinely declinable and some are embedded in systems the
clinician does not run, and pretending otherwise is the easy lie in this
encounter. The strong version of this conversation ends with the clinician saying
who signs the note and who is accountable for the decision, which is the thing the
patient came for.

### Card 6 — The daughter with the phone

**Clinician.** Inpatient, day three. You are pre-rounding when the patient's
daughter arrives with a list of things "the AI said you missed" after running
last night's labs through a chatbot. She is loud, the patient is embarrassed, and
one item on her list — a drug interaction — is a fair point that the team should
check.

**Patient's daughter.** You are frightened and you have been up all night. You do
not trust the overnight team because nobody explained the plan. You are not
trying to practice medicine. **You will not volunteer** that what you actually
want is for someone to sit down and tell you what is happening.

**What is being tested.** Conceding the good point out loud, in front of the
patient, without ceding the frame — and doing it while the patient is
humiliated. This card is the hardest of the six and should be assigned to rooms
that are running well. Note whether the clinician ever addresses the patient
rather than negotiating over them.

## Observer rubric

One per encounter, filled in by the observer during the conversation, used to
lead the five-minute debrief. Three points per item: **not yet / partly / yes**.
The observer speaks first in debrief, the clinician second, the patient last —
the patient's account of how it felt is the most valuable data in the room and it
gets contaminated if the clinician explains their intentions first.

| # | The clinician… | Not yet | Partly | Yes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Found out what the patient already believed before correcting anything | | | |
| 2 | Responded to the feeling before delivering the explanation | | | |
| 3 | Said in plain language what the AI tool did and what a human did — separately | | | |
| 4 | Named who is accountable for the decision, without hedging | | | |
| 5 | Was honest about what they personally did not know about the tool | | | |
| 6 | Said what the patient can decline, and what they cannot | | | |
| 7 | Left the patient with one concrete thing to do next | | | |
| 8 | Avoided jargon a non-clinician would have to guess at | | | |

Items 1, 2, and 5 are the ones students score worst on, and item 5 is the one
that predicts whether patients keep trusting the clinician after the visit.

**Free text, required, two lines only:**

> One thing to keep: ...
> One thing to change: ...

Not every item applies to every card — item 6 is irrelevant in Card 3, item 4 is
the whole game in Card 5. Mark inapplicable items with a dash rather than forcing
a score.

## The disclosure spine

Teach this once, in the didactic block, as a shape rather than a script.
Three sentences, in this order, covering the great majority of AI disclosure
conversations:

> **What it did.** "The software marked an area on your scan for a closer look."
> **What a person did.** "Dr. Chen read the scan herself and agreed the area
> needs another picture."
> **Who is responsible.** "The decision to bring you back is hers, and mine — not
> the software's."

The order matters more than the wording. Students who lead with accountability
sound defensive; students who never get to it leave the patient believing a
machine decided. Students should write their own three sentences for their
assigned card before the first rotation and then discover in the encounter how
little survives contact with an actual person.

## Framing notes

**Disclosure is genuinely unsettled, and say so.** There is no consensus rule
about which AI involvement requires telling the patient. Ambient documentation
that records the visit is not the same as decision support that never touches the
patient's awareness, and neither is the same as an assisted image read. Teach the
distinctions and teach that the profession has not finished arguing about them.
Fourth-year students are weeks from being the person in the room, and pretending
there is a settled rule sets them up to be wrong with confidence.

**The empathy finding is a trap if taught carelessly.** Ayers et al. is not
evidence that chatbots should answer patient messages. It is evidence about what
rushed physicians produce under time pressure, measured by blinded evaluators
reading text — not patients, not in a relationship, not over time. Let the
students sit with the discomfort of it, then make them articulate what the study
does not show. That is AIM-2 work smuggled into an AIM-6 session, and it is
worth the two minutes.

**Do not let this become a session about tools.** The competency is a
communication competency. A student who cannot explain a modality they have never
used is in exactly the position they will be in during intern year, when tools
appear in their workflow without warning. Item 5 on the rubric exists for that
reason: "I don't know how that system works, and here is how I'll find out" is a
correct, professional answer, and most students need permission to give it.

## Reusable material

The `ai-safety` deck — "Constructive Use of AI in the Life of a Medical
Resident," IDPT 8079, October 2025, tracked in the talks repo as a Drive-only
stub at `../talks/talks/ai-safety/` with its content recovered in
`../research/talks-drive-decks.md` — supplies two directly liftable pieces. Its
*keep transparency intact* slide (cite AI use; use AI for preparation of work,
not as a substitute for the work) is the professional-habit framing for this
session, and its **DEFT-AI** debrief structure — Diagnosis/Discussion, Evidence,
Feedback, Teaching, AI recommendation — is a ready-made alternative debrief
scaffold if the observer rubric proves too heavy for a given cohort. That deck is
already pitched at fourth-years and already built around two breakouts, so its
facilitation notes transfer without adaptation.

`../talks/archive/2024-10-23-ai-in-medical-education/` (published at
`https://talks.seandavis.net/archive/2024-10-23-ai-in-medical-education/`) carries
the student-attitudes and ethics material and the four-tier taxonomy of AI
policies, which is useful background for the frame but too faculty-facing to show
students directly.

For Card 1, the PHI and trust section at
`../talks/talks/agentic-coding/venues/2026-07-28-umihc.qmd:560-616` gives the
accurate account of consumer tiers, BAAs, and institutional deployments that the
clinician role needs in order to not invent a data-retention answer. It reads as
prose and lifts nearly verbatim.

For the accountability thread running through Cards 2, 5, and 6,
`../talks/archive/2024-10-28-ai-ml-oncology-service-line/` has the eight guiding
principles for AI tools in healthcare (doi:10.1038/s43856-023-00279-9), of which
*facilitate shared decision-making* and *incorporate biography* are the two that
belong in a patient-communication session. Its stakeholder-by-stakeholder
breakdown — patients, providers, researchers, administrators — is also the
clearest available answer to a student who asks who actually decided to deploy
the tool.

If a cohort pushes hard on "does this thing work for people like me,"
`../talks/archive/2024-11-05-ml-fairness/` is the seminar-ready companion, and
that question belongs to `core-bias-equity` rather than here. Redirect it rather
than absorbing it; this session has enough to do.
