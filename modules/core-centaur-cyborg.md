# Core module — Centaur or cyborg: constructive use in residency

**Format:** one 90-minute Zoom session · **Prereq:** none
**Competencies:** AIM-5 (clinical application and practical use); AIM-X (professional identity and adaptability); AIM-4 (ethics and professionalism)

## The claim this module tests

Every other session in this course teaches students to evaluate something. This
one asks them to commit to a position: for each task they will actually do as an
intern in a few months, how much of it belongs to the machine, and what do they
lose if they hand it over. The framing is deliberately not "should you use AI" —
that argument is over, and the students know it. It is *what kind of use, for
which task, at what risk*.

The sharp instrument is the distinction between skills you lose, skills you
never acquire, and skills you acquire wrongly. A fourth-year on the edge of
internship is at maximum exposure to the middle one: they are about to learn
clinical reasoning for the first time, with a tool available that will produce a
plausible-looking answer to every question they were supposed to struggle with.
The session's honest position is that the tool is worth using anyway, and that
using it well is a skill that has to be taught rather than assumed.

Built on the recovered `ai-safety` deck, "Constructive Use of AI in the Life of a
Medical Resident," which rests on Abdulnour RE, Gin B, Boscardin CK,
*Educational Strategies for Clinical Supervision of Artificial Intelligence Use*,
N Engl J Med 2025;393(8):786–797.

## Learning objectives

By the end, a student can:

1. Distinguish deskilling, never-skilling, and mis-skilling, and name one task in their own coming internship at risk from each.
2. Define the centaur and cyborg modes of AI use and classify a concrete clinical task into one, with a stated justification tied to task risk and tool validation.
3. Defend a classification against a peer who sorted the same task the other way, and revise it or not on stated grounds.
4. Construct a prompt using the Who / Where / What scaffold and convert an answer-seeking prompt into a hint-seeking one.
5. State what the randomized study of AI-assisted learning found about short-term performance versus knowledge gain and transfer, and name two techniques that counteract the effect.
6. Answer the AIM-X question in writing: which part of clinical judgment they will not delegate, and why.

## Tool and prep notes

**Pre-work is one account, a week ahead.** Students need a working login on the
course's standard assistant. Nothing else is required, and the session is
survivable without it — the sorting exercise is a judgment task, not a tool task.

**No PHI, ever, and here it needs saying out loud rather than pinning to chat.**
This session is where students form the habits they will carry into a hospital
with a real EHR beside them, and the temptation named by the students
themselves is time pressure. Free and consumer tiers carry no BAA. Every example
in this session is fictional and written by the student.

**Instructor honesty is load-bearing here.** Abdulnour et al. note that
supervisors often have less AI expertise than their learners, and that the
recommended posture is shared inquiry rather than authority. A facilitator who
performs expertise they do not have will lose the room, and the room in this
case includes people who use these tools more than the faculty do. Say so in the
first two minutes.

## Session plan (90 min)

| Time | Mode | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8 | Frame | Open on the anonymous student quote from a prior cohort: comparing one's own thought process against an AI response to evaluate *both* the medical knowledge and the AI, "as if the program is a mentor or learner" — followed by the same student's admission that they do not yet trust their ability to prompt well and feel "the temptation to quickly use AI to save time despite the limitations." State the scope: LLMs in the everyday life of a busy resident, teacher, and care-team member, being integrated into systems you do not control, used with increasing regularity, less supervision, and more autonomy. |
| 8–18 | Breakout A (4–5) | Discuss with your colleagues: what are your concerns about using AI for your own work? Each group posts one sentence into the chat before rooms close. No slides, no reading, no warm-up — this is first because participation earned in minute nine is participation you still have in minute seventy. |
| 18–34 | Talk, seeded by the chat | Read three or four of the posted concerns aloud, then answer them with the faculty provocations (below). Then the pitfalls: overtrust in plausible answers, poor prompts, ignoring the reasoning, uncritical acceptance, using AI for graded or sensitive work. Then the three perils, slowly: **deskilling** (losing reasoning skills you had), **never-skilling** (never learning them at all), **mis-skilling** (learning incorrect reasoning patterns). Close the block with the randomized-study finding on metacognitive laziness. |
| 34–44 | Talk + live demo | Centaur vs. cyborg. Then the two things that make the next breakout tractable: the **Who / Where / What** prompting scaffold, demonstrated live on a real prompt, and the five anti-laziness techniques. |
| 44–68 | Breakout B (same groups) | The eight-task sort — see below. This is the session's artifact. |
| 68–82 | Report-back and cross-group comparison | Grid the eight tasks against the groups' verdicts on one slide. Spend the time on the disagreements, not the consensus, and spend most of it on drafting vs. writing a progress note. |
| 82–90 | Close | DEFT-AI debrief, then the AIM-X question in writing. |

**Deliverable:** each group submits an eight-row grid — task, centaur or cyborg,
one-sentence justification, and one flagged disagreement they could not resolve
— plus one prompt rewritten with the Who/Where/What scaffold. Each student
individually submits one sentence naming what they will not delegate. The grids
are compared live; the individual sentences are collected and reread in the
final session of the course.

## The faculty provocations

These are attributed quotes from colleagues and should stay attributed — the
point is partly that experienced physicians disagree with each other in public.
Use them to answer the concerns the students just posted, not as a lecture
block.

- "There is one right approach or answer" — flagged as not reality except in
  the simplest cases; "medicine is not about the black and white."
  (Christina Olson, MD)
- "We should be scared. Because what we do is scary!" (Christina Olson, MD)
- From the ChangeMedEd conference: five to ten years away from *not* using AI
  being malpractice — "You CAN choose to avoid AI use, but is that really an
  option?" (Chad Stickrath, MD)
- Lectures only get you so far; competence comes from doing, whether that is
  taking a history, using a stethoscope, talking about death, or using AI.
  (Matthew Zuckerman, MD)
- "AI is new and powerful, but new is the rule, not the exception" — the
  internet, the EHR, and patient chat messages were all new once. (Sean Davis)

## Centaur and cyborg

A **centaur** divides the work and verifies the output: the clinician assigns a
bounded piece to the tool, keeps the rest, and evaluates what comes back. A
**cyborg** co-creates, moving back and forth with the tool inside a single piece
of work. Neither is the correct mode. Adaptive use means knowing when to shift,
and the shift happens moment to moment by task type and risk.

Centaur strategies suit high-stakes tasks and tools not validated for the use
you are putting them to: delegate carefully, always evaluate the output. Cyborg
strategies may genuinely improve efficiency on low-risk, creative, or
well-validated tasks. The underlying capacity Abdulnour et al. name is *adaptive
expertise* — the ability to shift between efficient routine practice and
innovative problem-solving — and they are explicit that critical thinking is its
foundation and has to be taught, modeled, and assessed *during* AI use rather
than assumed to survive it.

## Activity — the eight-task sort (24 min)

Groups of 4–5. Each group works the same eight tasks, in this order, and must
reach a verdict on every one:

1. Drafting a progress note
2. Writing a progress note
3. Literature search
4. Differential diagnosis creation
5. Patient education handout
6. Slide presentation for journal club
7. Summarizing an academic paper
8. Answering a patient message

The draft/write pairing is deliberate and is where most of the learning is. A
group that gives both the same verdict has usually not yet noticed that one of
them is a task with a human already holding the pen and the other is not.

For each task the group states the verdict, one sentence of justification, and
the supporting steps it would actually take: specify the task, ask for the
reasoning, find supporting evidence, evaluate the output, apply judgment,
integrate reflectively. Groups do not have to agree internally — an unresolved
disagreement, recorded as a disagreement with both positions written down, is a
better artifact than a forced consensus, and the report-back is more useful when
some groups bring one.

Last five minutes of the breakout: take whichever task the group rated most
cyborg-appropriate and write one prompt for it using Who / Where / What. The
scaffold, with the worked openings from the source deck:

- **Who** — "I am a fourth-year medical student…"
- **Where** — "I am on a rotation in X in a hospital setting…"
- **What** — "I want to understand why my patient is being treated using…" or
  "I want to teach my medical students about disease X."

## Metacognitive laziness and the five techniques

The uncomfortable finding to put in front of the room before the sort, not
after: a randomized experimental study comparing learners across AI,
human-expert, and checklist-tool conditions (doi:10.1111/bjet.13544) found that
ChatGPT **significantly improved short-term task performance but did not
correspondingly improve intrinsic motivation, knowledge gain, or transfer**, and
that it promoted dependence and triggered what the authors call metacognitive
laziness — offloading the monitoring and evaluation of one's own thinking to the
tool. The finding is not that the tool does not work. It is that the thing it
improves and the thing a learner needs are not the same thing.

The deck's answer to "should we use LLMs to help us learn?" is *probably, yes* —
with five techniques. The source material works these in R and dplyr; the
clinical substitutions are straightforward and should be made before delivery:

1. **Start on your own.** Build your differential before you ask. Grappling is
   the part that does the learning; consult when genuinely stuck, not when
   merely uncertain.
2. **Ask for hints, not answers.** Not "give me the differential for this
   presentation," but "I have anchored on a cardiac cause and I think I am
   missing something in the abdomen — what categories should I be considering
   that I have not named?"
3. **Teach back.** State your reasoning in your own words and ask whether your
   understanding is correct, rather than asking for the reasoning to be supplied.
4. **Focus on the why.** Ask why this drug and not that one, why this cutoff,
   why this is the first-line agent. Understanding the reason is what makes the
   knowledge portable to the next patient.
5. **Use the four patterns deliberately** — explanation of something you already
   read, debugging your own reasoning after it failed, conceptual clarification,
   and learning a better approach *after* you have already solved the problem
   yourself.

The sixth, from the safety deck and worth adding because it inverts the usual
posture: **ask the AI to quiz you** on your understanding. The mode where the
tool tests you is the one least likely to produce dependence.

## The close — DEFT-AI (8 min)

Debrief the session with the structure students should carry into supervision
conversations in residency, which is also how their future supervisors are being
taught to debrief them:

- **D**iagnosis / Discussion — what was your reasoning, and how did AI assist?
- **E**vidence — how did you verify?
- **F**eedback — what could improve?
- **T**eaching — what did you learn about AI and about reasoning?
- **AI** recommendation — what are the next steps for safe use?

Then the individual written question, which is the AIM-X thread and gets
collected: *what part of clinical judgment will you not delegate, and why?*
Students keep a copy. The final session of the course asks them to reread it and
say whether they still believe it.

The source deck closes on the line worth keeping: "You are the leaders — not
even the future ones."

## Honest gaps in the source material

Two flags carried forward from `../research/talks-drive-decks.md`:

**SAFE-AI is not recoverable.** The source deck names both SAFE-AI and DEFT-AI
as frameworks but only expands DEFT-AI in the extractable slide text; SAFE-AI
appears as a titled slide with no text, most likely a figure. This module
therefore uses DEFT-AI only. If SAFE-AI matters to your delivery, open the
original Drive file — do not reconstruct it from the acronym.

**Speaker notes were not extracted from any of the recovered decks.** The
on-slide structure is reliable; the connective narrative is not in the summary.
The provocations in particular are quotes without their surrounding argument.

**The `llm-in-medicine` deck is mislabeled** in the talks repo — its stub calls
it "How Large Language Models Learn," but the deck is *"The Augmented Analyst:
Integrating LLMs into your R journey without Sacrificing Critical Thinking"* and
contains nothing about how LLMs are trained. The metacognitive-laziness material
used above comes from that deck under its real title. Anyone searching the repo
by descriptor will not find it.
