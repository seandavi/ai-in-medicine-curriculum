# Weekly Resource Log

**AI in Medicine elective — four-week full-time block**

This document describes one course component: the weekly self-directed resource assignment, lightly restructured from previous years. Students still choose their own resources on whatever interests them — learning materials, primary literature, or broader topics such as ethics, equity, environmental impact, policy, or the history of the field. What changes is that each entry answers three short prompts instead of being a free summary, and week 4 turns the accumulated reading into a short synthesis rather than more reading.

Four parts:

1. Directions for the instructor
2. Learning objectives
3. Instructions for students (ready to paste into the LMS)
4. Optional: LLM/chatbot integration with faded prompts

---

## Part 1 — Directions for the instructor

### What changes from previous years, and what does not

Kept as-is: weekly cadence, 3–5 resources per week, roughly 3–5 sentences per resource, complete freedom of topic.

Changed:

- **Each entry answers three fixed prompts** (what it says, what it changed for you, what it left open). Same length as before; the prompts just push the sentences from summary toward reflection.
- **Optional breadth nudge.** Students are encouraged, not required, to touch at least two or three of the listed theme areas across the block.
- **Week 4 is synthesis, not reading.** Students pick one theme that emerged across their weeks 1–3 entries and write a short piece about it, then present it in the last small group.
- **Light AI-use note** each week. One or two sentences on whether and how they used a chatbot and what it got wrong, if anything. This is deliberately low-effort; its purpose is to make the tools discussable, not to police them.

Grading load is unchanged or slightly lower, since week 4 replaces 3–5 entries with one short piece.

### Timeline

| Week | Deliverable | Small-group tie-in |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3–5 resources, three prompts each, AI-use note | Each student names one resource and one surprise |
| 2 | 3–5 resources, three prompts each, AI-use note | What connected to last week; what contradicted it |
| 3 | 3–5 resources, three prompts each, AI-use note | Which theme are you choosing for week 4, and why |
| 4 | One-page synthesis on a theme + 5-minute presentation | Presentations |

If you keep only two small-group sessions, use weeks 1 and 4.

### What to prepare

- A short menu of theme areas (below, in the student instructions) with two or three starter resources in each, clearly marked as optional. Students who already know what they want will ignore it; students who stall will use it.
- The entry template posted as a file.
- One exemplar week written by you — three entries showing the three prompts done well, including one entry on something non-technical (an ethics essay, a piece on energy use) so students see that breadth is welcome.

### Grading

Complete/incomplete, with brief feedback in weeks 1 and 2 while habits form.

| Criterion | Meets | Falls short |
|---|---|---|
| Resources chosen | Specific, locatable, varied across the block | Only general news or only one type |
| Prompt 1 (what it says) | Accurate main point in one or two sentences | Restated title or abstract |
| Prompt 2 (what it changed) | Names a specific belief, question, or assumption that shifted or sharpened | "It was interesting" |
| Prompt 3 (what it left open) | A real question or something to verify | "More research is needed" |
| Week 4 synthesis | Draws on at least three of their own earlier entries | Disconnected from weeks 1–3 |

### Running the small groups

Keep them small. Ask each student one question: "What is the most interesting disagreement you have found so far?" By week 3 most students will have one; those who do not need a nudge toward a second theme area.

## Part 2 — Learning objectives

By the end of the block, students will be able to:

1. **Find and select** resources on AI in medicine from a range of source types — tutorials and courses, primary literature, reviews, policy and regulatory documents, and essays on ethics, equity, environment, or economics.
2. **State** the main point of a resource accurately and concisely, distinguishing what it shows from what it argues.
3. **Articulate** how a resource changed, sharpened, or challenged their own prior understanding.
4. **Identify** an open question or unverified claim that a resource leaves behind.
5. **Synthesize** their own reading across a block into a short piece on one theme, drawing connections and disagreements among sources.
6. **Use AI chat tools reflectively** as a reading aid, noticing where they help and where they mislead.

## Part 3 — Instructions for students

> The text below is written to be pasted directly into the LMS.

### Overview

Each week for three weeks you will choose 3–5 resources on AI in medicine and write a short structured entry on each. In week 4 you will write one short synthesis on a theme that emerged from your reading and present it in small group.

The topic is yours. Resources can be learning materials (a course module, a tutorial, a textbook chapter, a lecture video), primary literature, reviews, regulatory or policy documents, or pieces on broader questions — ethics, bias and equity, environmental cost, economics, the effect on medical training, the history of the field, patient perspectives. Mixing types is encouraged.

### Theme areas (optional guide)

You do not have to follow this list. It exists so that no one is stuck for a starting point, and so you can see the range of what counts.

- How the technology works (ML fundamentals, LLMs, foundation models)
- Clinical evidence (validation studies, trials, deployment reports)
- Ethics, bias, and equity
- Regulation and policy (FDA, EU AI Act, institutional governance)
- Environmental and economic impact
- Effects on medical education and the profession
- Patient perspectives and communication
- History and hype cycles

Over the block, try to touch at least two or three areas. Starter resources for each are posted separately.

### Weeks 1–3

Each week, choose 3–5 resources. For each one, complete the entry template. Then add a short AI-use note for the week.

Week 2 suggestion: include at least one resource that connects to, or disagrees with, something from week 1.

Week 3 suggestion: by the end of the week, know which theme you will write about in week 4. Small group that week will ask.

### Entry template (one per resource)

```
Resource:            (citation, URL, or title and where to find it)
Type:                (tutorial / primary study / review / policy /
                      essay or opinion / other)

1. What it says     — The main point, in one or two sentences.
                      What does it show or argue?

2. What it changed  — One or two sentences on what this shifted,
                      sharpened, or challenged in how you think
                      about the topic. Be specific.

3. What it left open — One question you now have, or one claim you
                      would want to check before repeating it.
```

Three to five sentences per entry is about right. Longer is fine if you have something to say; padding is not.

### AI-use note (one per week, two or three sentences)

Did you use a chatbot or LLM this week to find, read, or summarize resources? If so, what for, and did it get anything wrong or invent anything? If not, that is fine — just say so.

You are welcome to use these tools. Any reference a chatbot gives you must be verified before you list it; treat an unverified reference as made up.

### Week 4 — Synthesis

No new resources. Choose one theme that ran through your weeks 1–3 entries. Write a short piece (roughly 300–500 words) that:

- names the theme and why it caught your attention,
- draws on at least three of your own earlier entries, noting where they agreed, disagreed, or built on each other,
- ends with what you now think, and what you would still want to know.

Prepare a 5-minute version for small group. Slides optional.

### Submission

One document per week, due [day/time]. Plain text or PDF. Templates may be copied verbatim.

## Part 4 (optional) — LLM/chatbot integration with faded prompts

### The idea

Offer students a chatbot configured as a *reading companion* — something that helps them find resources, talk through what they read, and get to the three prompts — with the scaffolding reduced each week. Week 1 the bot leads the conversation; by week 4 it has no instructions and students decide how to use it. The AI-use note becomes a place to reflect on that shift.

The bot is never the author of the entries. It asks, suggests, and challenges.

### Three delivery levels

**Level 1 — Posted prompts.** Post each week's prompt as a text file; students paste it into any chatbot. No infrastructure. You cannot see transcripts.

**Level 2 — Shared assistant.** A Claude Project, custom GPT, or similar with the weekly system prompt and the entry template attached. Share the link; swap the prompt weekly. Simplest option that gives every student the same experience.

**Level 3 — Course chatbot.** A small app you control with per-week prompts, transcript logging (with consent), and a "flag this as wrong" button. Only worth it if you expect to run the course for several years or want the transcripts.

### The four weekly prompts

Written as system prompts. For Level 1, post with the heading "Paste this at the start of your conversation."

**Week 1 — Bot leads**

```
You are a reading companion for a medical student in an elective on
AI in medicine. The student chooses their own resources on any
topic — technical, clinical, ethical, policy, environmental, or
otherwise. Your job is to help them think, not to write for them.

When the student brings a resource, walk through three questions,
one at a time, asking for their answer before offering your own
view (clearly labeled as yours):
  1. What is the main point — what does it show or argue?
  2. What did this change or sharpen in how you think about the topic?
  3. What question does it leave open, or what would you want to check?

When the student asks for resources: suggest where to look and what
to search for. If you name a specific paper, book, or site, say
clearly that it must be verified, because you may be wrong about
titles, authors, or whether it exists.

Do not draft entries the student can paste. Keep replies short and
end each resource with one thing you are unsure about.
```

**Week 2 — Bot connects**

```
You are a reading companion for a medical student in week 2 of an
elective on AI in medicine. The student already knows the three
questions (main point, what it changed, what it left open); do not
list them unless asked.

This week, when the student brings a resource, ask what they read
last week and help them find a connection or a disagreement between
the two. Prefer disagreements — they are more useful.

Same rules as before: do not draft entries; any specific reference
you name must be verified by the student; end each resource with one
thing you are unsure about.
```

**Week 3 — Bot challenges**

```
You are a discussion partner for a medical student in week 3 of an
elective on AI in medicine. The student will tell you what they have
been reading and which theme they are considering for a short
synthesis next week.

Your job this week is to push back. For each claim the student
repeats from their reading, ask: how do they know that, what kind of
source it came from, and whether any of their other reading cuts
against it. Do not summarize sources for them; ask them to summarize
to you.

Do not write the synthesis or an outline for it. Any specific
reference you name must be verified.
```

**Week 4 — No scaffold**

```
You are a general assistant. You have no special instructions about
the student's assignment. Two standing rules: you will not write
their synthesis, and you will not give references they have not
verified.
```

Post with a one-line note: *This week the prompt is deliberately empty. Your AI-use note should say how you chose to use the tool, or not, and why.*

### Making the AI-use note do some work

- In week 1, invite students to ask the bot for three resources on their topic and check all three. Most cohorts will find at least one that does not exist or is misattributed. That is the lesson, and it costs ten minutes.
- In weeks 2–3, the interesting notes are the ones where the bot's summary of something and the student's own reading diverged. Ask about those in small group.
- In week 4, one sentence in the synthesis on what role AI tools played is enough, and mirrors the disclosure now expected by journals.

### What to tell students up front

- The bot is told not to write your entries. Getting it to anyway defeats the point of the course.
- Verify every reference it gives you.
- Not using it at all is a legitimate choice; say so in the note.
