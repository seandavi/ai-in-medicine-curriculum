# What medical students want to learn about AI, and what worries them

Prepared 2026-10-07 by Claude Code (Opus 5.5) at the course director's request.
This is a design file, not student-facing content. It has not been checked by
a person yet. Every PMID below was looked up in PubMed on 2026-10-07, and its
title, journal, year, and abstract were read. Full text was read only where
the evidence table says so.

## 1. Question and method

**Question.** What do medical students say they most want to learn about AI?
What are they most interested in, and most worried about? How well does this
book cover those topics?

**Priorities set in the brief.** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses first.
Then large or multi-school surveys, US studies, and surveys from 2023 onward.
Then surveys of students in clinical years or recent graduates. A few older
surveys are included only because they are widely cited.

**Searches run in PubMed on 2026-10-07.** Counts are the totals PubMed
reported. "Screened" means the abstract was read.

## 2. What the surveys say

### The overall picture

Across reviews and large surveys, the same pattern repeats. Students are
broadly positive about AI. They want it taught. They feel they know little
and are not ready to use it. They worry most about wrong answers, ethics and
law, and, for some specialties, their future jobs.

The largest synthesis is a 2026 meta-analysis of 96 surveys from 37 countries,
with more than 45,000 medical students [@pmid:42612090]. Its pooled estimates
were: positive attitude toward AI 76.9%; support for teaching AI in the
curriculum 76.6%; willingness to learn about or adopt AI 71.5%; ethical
concerns 62.8%; trust in AI-assisted decisions 50.6%; concern about doctors
being replaced 39.9%. The authors rated every one of these as very low
certainty. The range of likely values in a new setting was very wide. For
replacement concern it ran from 6.6% to 80.1%. So these are rough averages,
not stable facts about students everywhere.

### Interests and learning needs

**1. "Teach us this." Very consistent.** Nearly every survey and review found
that most students want AI in the curriculum. A 2026 meta-analysis of 26
studies (20,963 students) found 83.1% agreed AI training is needed. Only
36.4% felt confident applying AI in practice [@pmid:42310669]. The authors
call this an "optimism-competence gap". The global survey of 4,596 students
found 76.1% wanted more AI teaching, and 76.3% had no AI teaching at all
[@pmid:39342231]. Older surveys found the same: 78% in the UK [@pmid:32025951],
91.5% in the US [@pmid:36269641], 85.6% in a 63-country survey
[@pmid:35004598].

**2. What to teach. Fewer studies, but they point the same way.** Only a few
surveys asked students to rank topics.

- In a 2021 US survey (390 students, 17 schools), the top topics were
  fundamental concepts of AI (65.2%), when to use AI in medicine (59.9%), and
  pros and cons of AI (59.1%) [@pmid:36269641].
- In the global survey, practical skills ranked first. Future outlook came
  next, then legal and ethical aspects [@pmid:39342231]. The paper reports this
  order in the discussion; the exact percentages are in its table, which I
  did not extract.
- In Germany, Austria, and Switzerland (487 students, 2023), every proposed
  AI ethics topic was rated highly relevant [@pmid:38180787].
- In a 2025 US convenience sample (552 students, at least 37 schools), 64.2%
  wanted more training or clearer guidance. Only 50.3% felt confident
  evaluating AI-generated content [@pmid:42779013].
- In a 2025 Canadian national survey (286 students, 10 schools), only 21.43%
  felt adequately educated on data privacy rules for these tools
  [@pmid:42400960].

**3. Format.** US students in 2021 preferred short lectures (69.8%), formal
electives (47.6%), and question-and-answer panels (44.2%) [@pmid:36269641]. A
review of 19 studies reported that students favour interactive practice over
lectures [@pmid:41973159]. For radiology and AI, students preferred to learn
during radiology rotations [@pmid:34619566] or from expert opinion
[@pmid:30424998].

**4. What students already use AI for.** This is not a ranked "interest". It
shows what they would bring to a course. The most common use is studying.

- US, 2024 (177 students, 14 schools): understanding complex concepts,
  exam preparation, and making study materials. 46.5% of users had used it for
  assignments. 21.0% had used it to help write clinical notes. The most common
  clinical use was generating a differential diagnosis [@pmid:41802232].
- North America, May 2023 (415 students, at least 28 schools): explaining
  concepts in preclinical years; help with diagnosis and treatment plans in
  clerkship [@pmid:39236008].
- Canada, 2025: 96.50% used at least one tool. ChatGPT (93.36%) and
  OpenEvidence (57.69%) were the most common [@pmid:42400960].
- China, 2025 (1,062 students, 168 schools): course learning (71.8%) far more
  than clinical decisions (44.0%) [@pmid:41645229].

### Concerns

**1. Wrong answers. Very consistent, and now from direct experience.** In
Canada, 90.18% of students had met inaccurate output [@pmid:42400960]. In
Ontario, 91.6% recognised the risk of inaccuracy [@pmid:41181167]. In China,
more than 65% named accuracy as an urgent problem [@pmid:42021208]. US
students listed inaccurate responses first among limitations
[@pmid:39236008].

**2. Ethics and law. Consistent.** The pooled rate of ethical concern was 62.8%
[@pmid:42612090]. In the global survey, 69.4% agreed AI will lead to more
legal and ethical conflicts [@pmid:39342231]. In the US 2025 sample, 78.7%
reported academic integrity or ethical concerns, or both [@pmid:42779013].
Reviews name bias in algorithms and unclear accountability among the main
concerns [@pmid:42183461].

**3. Jobs and specialty choice. Common, but highly variable.** This concern is
strongest for radiology, where most of the evidence sits.

- A 2023 meta-analysis of 21 studies found 22.36% of respondents were less
  likely to choose radiology because of AI. The rate was higher in medical
  students (31.94%) than in radiology trainees and radiologists (9.16%)
  [@pmid:37349157].
- In the US (32 schools), one in six students who would have ranked radiology
  first did not, because of AI [@pmid:34619566]. The same one-in-six figure
  appeared in an earlier Canadian national survey [@pmid:30424998].
- In the UK, 49% said AI made them less likely to consider radiology
  [@pmid:32025951]. In a 2026 Canadian survey, 32.9% reported being
  discouraged from radiology by AI [@pmid:42191531].
- Beyond radiology, 52.6% of Turkish students expected AI to influence their
  specialty choice [@pmid:42381025]. In Lebanon the figure was 26.8%
  [@pmid:36406470].
- Students who knew more about AI or radiology were less anxious in several
  surveys [@pmid:30424998] [@pmid:36082861]. These are associations, not
  evidence that teaching reduces anxiety.
- Not all surveys agree. A widely cited German survey found 83% disagreed that
  radiologists would be replaced [@pmid:29980928].

Pathology and dermatology appear in reviews as specialties students link with
AI [@pmid:38622577]. I found no large student survey focused on either.

**4. Academic integrity. Under-measured, but present when asked.** In China,
48.6% feared plagiarism accusations [@pmid:42443861]. In Ukraine, 36% called AI
use misconduct, 26% called it acceptable, and 38% were undecided
[@pmid:41146155]. US students named plagiarism as a limitation
[@pmid:39236008]. The split in Ukraine suggests students are unsure where the
line is, not only worried about crossing it.

**5. Privacy.** US students named patient privacy as a limitation
[@pmid:39236008]. The low privacy-training figure in Canada (21.43%) is in
section 2 above [@pmid:42400960]. The authors of the 2024 US study raised
concern that 21.0% had used ChatGPT to help write clinical notes
[@pmid:41802232].

**6. Over-reliance and lost critical thinking.** In China, 43.0% worried about
over-reliance [@pmid:42443861]. A review of Indian studies named over-reliance
and reduced critical thinking as recurring concerns [@pmid:41480281].

**7. Bias.** In Ontario, 78.9% recognised the risk of bias [@pmid:41181167].
In China, 65.6% named susceptibility to input data bias [@pmid:41645229].

**8. Trust and explanation.** Pooled trust in AI-assisted decisions was 50.6%,
from only 8 studies [@pmid:42612090]. In the global survey, 80.2% preferred an
AI that explains itself over one that is more accurate [@pmid:39342231].

### Residency applications

No survey I read ranked residency applications as an interest or concern.
That is probably because no one asked. One small survey of 35 neurosurgery
interviewees found 26% used a language model for minor edits to the personal
statement, and none for major sections [@pmid:41552112]. Blinded studies found
that program reviewers could not reliably tell AI-written personal statements
from human ones [@pmid:42005879] [@pmid:40735658]. Those are studies of
reviewers, not of what students want.

### How strong is this evidence?

- Almost all studies are single-time surveys of self-report.
- Most use convenience samples with low or unknown response rates. The global
  survey estimated 0.2% of all enrolled students [@pmid:39342231]. The 2021 US
  survey estimated 3.5% [@pmid:36269641].
- Instruments differ, and many are not validated [@pmid:42612090].
- Students interested in AI are more likely to answer.
- US data are thin. The four US multi-school surveys since 2021 total about
  1,500 respondents, and none is a probability sample.
- Very few studies report senior students separately. The 2024 US study had
  52% with at least one clerkship block, but did not split results by year
  [@pmid:41802232].

## 5. Gaps and suggestions

These are suggestions only. No chapter, resource list, or lab was edited.

**1. Residency applications (missing; highest priority for fourth-years).**
Students in this course are writing applications in the same months. The
surveys did not ask about this, so the evidence is indirect. Suggestions:

- Add to starter resources: AMA News, "How to use AI in residency
  applications? Learn the rules of the road" (B. Murphy, 9 September 2026).
  It quotes the AAMC: AI is acceptable "for brainstorming, proofreading or
  editing the personal statement, but the final submission should represent
  your own work." Page checked 2026-10-07.
  <https://www.ama-assn.org/medical-students/preparing-residency/how-use-ai-residency-applications-learn-rules-road>
- Consider an open lab, "Edit, don't author": students draft a paragraph of
  their own, ask a chatbot only for edits, and mark which changes they keep
  and why. The blinded-reviewer studies above [@pmid:42005879]
  [@pmid:40735658] give a ready discussion point: if reviewers cannot tell,
  what makes a statement worth reading?

**2. Academic integrity and disclosure (thin).** The 36/26/38 split in Ukraine
[@pmid:41146155] suggests students do not know where the line is. Suggestions:

- Add to starter resources: AAMC, "Principles for the Responsible Use of
  Artificial Intelligence in and for Medical Education" (version 2.0, 31 July
  2025). Seven principles, including ethical and transparent use and data
  privacy. Page checked 2026-10-07.
  <https://www.aamc.org/about-us/mission-areas/medical-education/principles-ai-use>
  It is written for schools as much as for learners; say so in the entry.
- Consider a short section in the centaur-cyborg chapter, beside objective 5,
  on disclosure outside the hospital: coursework, research, and applications.

**3. Studying with AI (thin, but the most common real use).** Every recent
usage survey put studying first. The centaur-cyborg six techniques already
address this. Suggestions:

- Write out the "Write your own board question" open lab next, since it
  matches what students already do.
- Add one starter resource on learning with chatbots. I did not verify a
  specific item for this; the metacognitive laziness study the chapter cites
  [@doi:10.1111/bjet.13544] is a candidate, but I did not re-check its
  metadata in this pass.

**4. Specialty anxiety beyond radiology (thin).** Suggestions:

- In the workforce chapter's Go deeper, add the student-survey evidence so
  students see that the worry is common and measured: the US study
  [@pmid:34619566] and the meta-analysis [@pmid:37349157].
- Add a pathology item to the specialty-specific resources. I did not verify
  a candidate in this pass.

**5. Privacy (covered in the course, missing from resources).** The Canadian
finding that only 21.43% felt educated on privacy rules [@pmid:42400960]
supports keeping the patient-information page prominent. A starter resource
that points to it would help students who skip course pages.

**6. Practical skills in resources (thin).** The global survey ranked practical
skills first [@pmid:39342231]. The chapters deliver this. The starter list
has only two tool entries. More "Tools to try" entries would match demand.

**7. Use survey numbers in the book, carefully.** A chapter could cite the
"optimism-competence gap" (83.1% want training, 36.4% feel able)
[@pmid:42310669] as a reason the course exists. Any such sentence must say
these are self-report surveys with very low certainty [@pmid:42612090].

**8. Possible future work.** A short pre-course survey of our own students,
using two or three items from the published surveys, would tell us whether
our cohort matches. That would also add to the thin US evidence.

  in the abstract. Full text was not read.

  find it on an AAMC page. The AMA article quotes the AAMC guidance, and that
  is what section 5 relies on.
- **Topic ranking percentages in Busch and colleagues [@pmid:39342231]:** the
  order is stated in the discussion. The percentages are in a table that did
  not come through in the full-text tool.
- **Full texts not read** for every other study, including both large
  meta-analyses. Figures come from the abstracts.
- **Search 7 (129 records) and search 2 (43 records) were not screened.**
  US studies of senior students or graduates may be among them.
- **AAMC Graduation Questionnaire** data on AI were not checked.
- **The metacognitive laziness DOI** (10.1111/bjet.13544) is cited by the book
  already; I did not re-check it here.
- **Coverage map:** based on headings, objectives, and grep, not a full read of
  every chapter.
- **Ledger:** this file is not student-facing, so I did not add an
  `AI-LEDGER.md` row. If any suggestion above is adopted into a chapter or the
  resources page, that change needs its own row.
