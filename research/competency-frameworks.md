# Competency Frameworks for Teaching AI/LLMs to Medical Students

Research current as of August 2026. Literature sourced from PubMed; policy and
accreditation documents fetched from AAMC, AMA, LCME, and Macy Foundation.

## Executive summary

There is no single authoritative AI competency framework for undergraduate
medical education (UME) yet. The AAMC's national competency set — the document
most US schools are waiting on — is still in development, with a target release
of **fall 2026**. In the meantime, roughly a dozen published frameworks converge
strongly on the same content, so a curriculum can be built on that consensus now
and remapped to the AAMC set when it lands.

Three things changed the landscape in 2025:

1. **LCME proposed revised Standard 7**, whose new Element 7.2 explicitly names
   AI as required curricular content. This is the first accreditation lever.
2. **The AMA adopted AI literacy policy** (Nov 17, 2025) directing development
   of model learning objectives and curricular toolkits.
3. **The Macy Foundation conference** (with AAMC and ACGME) issued
   recommendations that put "implement AI curricula and competencies" first.

Adoption is already near-universal and mostly required, not elective: AAMC survey
data show schools with AI content grew from 88 (2023) to 140 (2024), and of those
140, **107 place it in the required curriculum**.

## 1. AAMC

### 1.1 AI Competencies Across the Learning Continuum (in development)

- **Source:** Association of American Medical Colleges, supported by the Josiah
  Macy Jr. Foundation
- **Launched:** July 2025 | **Target release of final report: fall 2026**
- **URL:** https://www.aamc.org/about-us/medical-education/ai-competencies
- **Status:** Not yet published. Currently in a modified Delphi process plus
  focus groups with a working group consensus exercise.
- **Scope:** Designed to span UME, GME, and CME.
- **Stated goals** (the only concrete content available): learners should
  "develop a shared foundation in core AI concepts; understand the ethical,
  legal, and professional implications of AI-enabled care; and are prepared to
  effectively use, evaluate, and collaborate around AI technologies."
- **Structural note:** It will be published in the AAMC **New and Emerging Areas
  in Medicine Competency Series**, the same series that produced the Quality
  Improvement/Patient Safety and Telehealth/Virtual Care reports. Those reports
  are a good structural template to design against — competencies are stated per
  learner level across the continuum.
- **Contact:** curricularinnovation@aamc.org

**Planning implication:** design your competency statements so they can be
crosswalked to the AAMC set when published, rather than waiting for it.

### 1.2 Principles for the Responsible Use of AI in and for Medical Education

- **Version 2.0, completed July 31, 2025** (v1.0 Jan 3, 2025)
- **URL:** https://www.aamc.org/about-us/mission-areas/medical-education/principles-ai-use

Seven principles (verbatim titles):

1. Maintain Human-Centered Focus
2. Ensure Ethical and Transparent Use
3. Provide Equal Access to AI
4. Foster Education, Training, and Continuing Professional Development
5. Develop Curricula Through Interdisciplinary Collaboration
6. Protect Data Privacy
7. Monitor and Evaluate

These are governance principles for how a school uses AI, not learner
competencies. Principle 3 (equal access) is the one most often overlooked in
course design — it constrains which tools you can require students to use.

### 1.3 AI Competencies for Medical Educators (CGEA)

- **Compiled by:** CGEA Faculty Development SIG, AI in Medical Education Workgroup
- **Committee:** Billings HA, Gaynier A, Hall ER, Hurtubise L, Lowry JE, Parikh P,
  Pylman S, Rush E, Suh B, Thomas TV
- **Date:** May 2025 (page last updated October 2025)
- **PDF:** https://www.aamc.org/media/86881/download?attachment
- **Landing page:** https://www.aamc.org/about-us/mission-areas/medical-education/advancing-ai-resource-collection/cgea-artificial-intelligence-framework-educators

This is a **faculty** framework, not a student one — but it is the most concrete
published US domain structure, and it is worth mirroring so faculty development
runs parallel to the student curriculum. Eight domains:

- **A. Understanding AI** — AI fundamentals; statistical properties and varied AI
  approaches to data; AI versus GenAI; AI terminology; LLMs involve human
  intervention; AI is an adjunct not a primary source; range of applications;
  strengths and weaknesses of various AI systems.
- **B. Working with AI** — prompt/communicate with AI for meaningful results;
  factors affecting AI accuracy; use AI for productivity; risks and unintended
  consequences of misuse.
- **C. Critical Appraisal of AI Outputs** — critique outputs against task purpose;
  data literacy; recognize when AI is not appropriate for a task; evaluate and
  assess capabilities of AI tools.
- **D. Ethical Use of AI** — follow academic-use guidelines; personal
  responsibility for fairness and equity; black-box nature of ML models and
  sources of data/output bias; how personal and structural biases affect inputs
  and outputs; cite and attribute AI contributions; transparency and informed
  consent with stakeholders; protect intellectual property (HIPAA, FERPA,
  protected human subject data); identify automation bias and preserve skills.
- **E. AI Possibilities in Medical Education** — curriculum development; learning
  activity development; assessment and evaluation; selection/admissions.
- **F. AI Enhanced Clinical Encounters** — develop curriculum about AI in
  healthcare for students.
- **G. Using AI in Research and Scholarship** — applications in research; ethical
  and responsible research use (including IRB requirements for AI-generated
  data); data collection and analysis; scholarly writing, attribution, authorship.
- **H. Continuous Professional Development in AI** — experimentation; assess
  knowledge gaps; create a learning plan; seek resources; reflect; share with
  colleagues.

Each domain is mapped to the 2025 IACAI Vision and Integration Framework
(Matrix I, Educator Focus), MedBiquitous, 2025 —
https://www.medbiq.org/media/7986/download

## 2. AMA

### 2.1 Policy: AI literacy in medical education

- **Adopted November 17, 2025**, AMA Interim Meeting of the House of Delegates
  (National Harbor, MD)
- **URL:** https://www.ama-assn.org/press-center/ama-press-releases/ama-adopts-policy-advance-ai-literacy-medical-education

Directives:

1. Develop and disseminate **model AI learning objectives and curricular
   toolkits** to guide foundational education on AI use in clinical practice.
2. Standardize AI training in medical school and expand AI-focused CME.
3. Collaborate with other medical organizations to recognize AI literacy elements.
4. Advocate for funding and faculty-development resources.

The press release does not enumerate specific literacy elements; it emphasizes
understanding how AI tools function, recognizing their limitations, and
appreciating clinical applications.

### 2.2 Terminology

The AMA House of Delegates uses **"augmented intelligence"** rather than
"artificial intelligence," framing AI's role as assistive — enhancing rather than
replacing human intelligence. Worth adopting deliberately (or deliberately not)
in your course title and learning objectives.

- Principles doc: https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/ama-ai-principles.pdf
- Augmented intelligence hub: https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/digital-health/augmented-intelligence-medicine

### 2.3 Educational resources

- **ChangeMedEd Artificial Intelligence in Health Care Series** — seven-part free
  module series on AMA Ed Hub. Topics include navigating ethical and legal
  considerations of AI in health care, practical applications of AI in the health
  system, and the use of AI in diagnosis. Targeted at medical students but
  suitable for residents and practicing physicians.
  https://www.ama-assn.org/education/changemeded-initiative/ai-medical-education
- *Medical Educator's Guide to Projects Leveraging Artificial Intelligence and
  Learning Analytics* (free PDF).
- *Advancing AI in medical education through ethics, evidence and equity.*

## 3. Accreditation status

### 3.1 LCME — proposed revised Standard 7 (the important one)

From *Strategic Re-visioning of the LCME Accreditation Standards, Take Two*
(LCME, presented Oct 8, 2025):
https://lcme.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/LCME_LSL_2025_10-8-25_FINAL.pdf

Proposed **Element 7.2 (Patient Care)**, verbatim:

> The medical education curriculum includes content and clinical experiences
> related to the diagnosis and treatment of disease. This content and these
> clinical experiences include education and experiential learning in the areas
> of acute and chronic care, end-of-life care, continuity of care, and
> rehabilitative care; **in the appropriate use of artificial intelligence and
> other emerging technologies in diagnosis and patient management**; and in the
> development and effective application of the skills of evidence-based critical
> judgment to solving clinical problems.

The LCME's own crosswalk lists "Artificial intelligence in diagnosis and patient
management" under **"Topics Not Explicitly Included in 2025-26 F&S"** — i.e. this
is new content.

**Status caveat:** the structure of new Standard 7 was approved *in principle* by
the LCME in February 2025 and subsequently revised. It is a proposed revision,
**not yet in force**; the current 2025-26 *Functions and Structure of a Medical
School* does not contain an AI element. Treat it as a strong signal of direction,
not a present requirement. Note also that the element requires **experiential**
learning, not just didactic content — that constrains course design.

### 3.2 ACGME

No binding AI-specific standards. ACGME co-sponsored the 2025 Macy conference,
which recommended modifying accreditation, licensing, and certification standards
to account for AI. GME lags UME in published pragmatic frameworks.

### 3.3 Macy Foundation conference recommendations

- **Citation:** Josiah Macy Jr. Foundation Conference on Artificial Intelligence
  in Medical Education: Proceedings and Recommendations. *Acad Med.*
  2025;100(9S Suppl 1):S4-S14.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000006099) (PMID 40424523)
- Webinar deck (June 12, 2025), sponsored by Macy Foundation and AAMC:
  https://aamc.org/media/84381/download?attachment=

Five recommendations with action steps (verbatim):

1. **Promote the implementation of safe and effective AI in medical education**
   — 1.1 Implement AI curricula and competencies; 1.2 Reevaluate foundational
   physician competencies for the AI era; 1.3 Modify accreditation, licensing,
   and certification standards and processes; 1.4 Support educators' use of AI to
   promote learning; 1.5 Facilitate personal and organizational adaptability;
   1.6 Create partnerships and collaborations.
2. **Implement co-produced, AI-enhanced teaching, learning, assessment, &
   evaluation approaches** — 2.1 Develop AI playgrounds; 2.2 Use AI to improve
   existing instructional strategies; 2.3 Adapt assessment programs to the AI era.
3. **Develop and leverage robust data ecosystems** — 3.1 Integrate data systems;
   3.2 Build data transparency into assessment; 3.3 Embrace competency-based and
   precision medical education; 3.4 Promote data-driven excellence in accreditation.
4. **Facilitate ethical AI governance frameworks and responsible utilization** —
   4.1 Create comprehensive AI frameworks and ethical guidelines; 4.2 Build a
   national best practices network.
5. **Advance scholarship on AI in medical education** — 5.1 Study AI in medical
   education; 5.2 Conduct participatory research; 5.3 Fund ambitious scholarship;
   5.4 Use reporting standards; 5.5 Enhance dissemination; 5.6 Support
   interdisciplinary collaboration and industry partnerships; 5.7 Engage in
   strategic visioning.

Panelists: Alison Whelan (WashU, moderator), Cornelius James (Michigan), Adam
Rodman (HMS), Robert Wachter (UCSF), Eva Aagaard (AAMC).

## 4. Published competency frameworks

*Sources in this section were retrieved from PubMed.*

### 4.1 Russell et al. — Competencies for the Use of AI-Based Tools by Health Care Professionals

The most-cited framework and the de facto reference standard in US work.

- Russell RG, Lovett Novak L, Patel M, Garvey KV, Craig KJT, Jackson GP, Moore D,
  Miller BM. *Acad Med.* 2023;98(3):348-356.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000004963) (PMID 36731054)
- **Method:** qualitative; semistructured interviews with 15 experts (2021),
  thematic analysis, expert feedback, research-team consensus.
- **Output:** 6 competency domains, 25 subcompetencies.

Domains (verbatim):

1. **Basic knowledge of AI** — explain what AI is and describe its health care
   applications.
2. **Social and ethical implications of AI** — explain how social, economic, and
   political systems influence AI-based tools and how these relationships impact
   justice, equity, and ethics.
3. **AI-enhanced clinical encounters** — carry out AI-enhanced clinical
   encounters that integrate diverse sources of information in creating
   patient-centered care plans.
4. **Evidence-based evaluation of AI-based tools** — evaluate the quality,
   accuracy, safety, contextual appropriateness, and biases of AI-based tools and
   their underlying data sets in providing care to patients and populations.
5. **Workflow analysis for AI-based tools** — analyze and adapt to changes in
   teams, roles, responsibilities, and workflows resulting from implementation of
   AI-based tools.
6. **Practice-based learning and improvement regarding AI-based tools** —
   participate in continuing professional development and practice-based
   improvement activities related to use of AI tools in health care.

Note domains 3, 5, and 6 map cleanly onto ACGME core competencies
(patient care, systems-based practice, practice-based learning), which makes this
framework easy to integrate into existing competency architecture.

### 4.2 Hunt et al. — "What the AI era doctor should know" (scoping review)

The most current and most comprehensive synthesis specific to UME. **This should
be your primary backbone document.**

- Hunt VM, Sprehe LK, de Lomba WC, Gameiro RR, Woite NL, Wade CG, Chen JH,
  Rougas SC, Fraser HSF, Sullivan AM, Fregni F. *npj Digit Med.* 2026;9(1).
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-02761-9) (PMID 42120544)
- **Method:** PRISMA-ScR scoping review; PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, ERIC;
  inception through July 28, 2025; no language restriction. 4,071 records
  screened → **54 studies from 22 countries**; 564 competency statements extracted
  verbatim and decomposed.
- **Output:** **7 domains, 37 competencies, 170 learning objectives.**

The seven domains:

1. AI ethics
2. AI law and regulation
3. AI professionalism in healthcare
4. Clinical applications of AI
5. Critical appraisal of AI output
6. Research and innovation in AI
7. Theory and foundations of AI

Recurring emphasis across the corpus: **ethicolegal oversight, critical appraisal
of AI outputs, and foundational understanding of AI methods and data.** Authors
caution that source studies were predominantly editorial/opinion rather than
empirical, and that the taxonomy needs stakeholder-based prioritization and
evaluation.

### 4.3 Lee et al. — Medical AI competencies for medical school graduates (South Korea)

The largest stakeholder-validated Delphi in UME, and the one that distinguishes
*essential* from *optional*.

- Lee YM, Kim S, Lee YH, Kim HS, Seo SW, Kim H, Kim KJ. *Acad Med.*
  2024;99(5):524-533. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/ACM.0000000000005618)
  (PMID 38207056)
- **Method:** initial Delphi with 28 medical AI experts (2022) → 42 competency
  items; national questionnaire to 1,955 participants (1,174 students, 781
  professors) across South Korean medical schools; second Delphi with 33 medical
  educators from 21 schools to separate essential from optional.
- **Output:** 6 domains, 36 competencies.

Domains (verbatim):

1. Understanding digital health and changes driven by AI
2. Fundamental knowledge and skills in medical AI
3. Ethics and legal aspects in the use of medical AI
4. Medical AI application in clinical practice
5. Processing, analyzing, and evaluating medical data
6. Research and development of medical AI

**Key finding for scoping your course:** competencies in domains 1-4 were largely
rated **essential**; a higher proportion of experts rated domains 5 and 6 (data
science, AI research and development) **optional**. This is the strongest
published evidence for making data science and model development an elective
extension rather than core.

### 4.4 Singla et al. — Canadian AI medical curriculum Delphi

- Singla R, Pupic N, Ghaffarizadeh SA, Kim C, Hu R, Forster BB, Hacihaliloglu I.
  *npj Digit Med.* 2024;7(1):323.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-024-01307-1) (PMID 39557985)
- **Method:** three-round Delphi, 18 Canadian health and AI experts.
- **Output:** **82 of 107 curricular competencies deemed essential.**
- **Competency areas:** ethics, law, theory, application, communication,
  collaboration, and quality improvement.
- **Distinctive value:** each curriculum element is mapped to AFMC **entrustable
  professional activities (EPAs)** and to **CanMEDS roles**. If you want an EPA
  mapping rather than a freestanding competency list, this is the model to copy.
  It also includes concrete suggestions for fitting content into an already-dense
  curriculum.

### 4.5 DECODE — Digital Health Competencies in Medical Education

Broader than AI, but the largest and most rigorous international consensus in the
adjacent space; AI sits inside its health data science domain.

- Car J, Ong QC, Erlikh Fox T, et al. *JAMA Netw Open.* 2025;8(1):e2453131.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.53131) (PMID 39888625)
- **Method:** scoping review + expert consultation → 2-round modified Delphi with
  **211 experts from 79 countries** (149 in round 2), 70% a priori consensus
  threshold, consensus meeting Sept 8, 2023, plus post-meeting feedback.
- **Output:** **4 domains, 19 competencies, 33 mandatory and 145 discretionary
  learning outcomes.**

Domains:

1. Professionalism in digital health
2. Patient and population digital health
3. Health information systems
4. Health data science

**Design idea worth stealing:** the mandatory/discretionary split for learning
outcomes. It solves the curriculum-space problem directly — a small mandatory
core with a large optional set schools adapt to local resources.

### 4.6 Gong et al. — Chinese expert consensus on AI proficiency (CAIP)

The most developed **assessment** framework of any published AI competency work.

- Gong M, Li J, Ma Y, Jin B, Chen W, Hou Y, Hong L, Lai T, Zhang B, Wu G, Zeng Z.
  *Health Care Sci.* 2026;5(1):49-57.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/hcs2.70049) (PMID 41767172)
- Companion Chinese-language version: Gong MC, Pan H, Liu H, et al.
  *Zhongguo Yi Xue Ke Xue Yuan Xue Bao.* 2026;48(1):13-23.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3881/j.issn.1000-503X.17210) (PMID 41645431)
- **Method:** 32-member multidisciplinary expert group; Knowledge-Skills-Attitude
  competency model; two Delphi rounds (100% response; consensus threshold mean
  ≥4.0, CV ≤0.25); Analytic Hierarchy Process to prioritize.
- **Output:** **21-item CAIP list** — 8 knowledge, 7 skills, 6 attitude
  indicators. (The Chinese-language edition reports 8/8/5.)

Top-prioritized competencies by AHP:

- CAIP3 — understanding AI's role in multidisciplinary knowledge integration
- CAIP4 — identifying AI output biases
- CAIP2 — understanding health data governance
- CAIP16 — maintaining physician-led AI-assisted diagnosis
- CAIP12 — identifying AI diagnostic biases

**Assessment model (the genuinely reusable part):**

- Knowledge → standardized paper/computerized testing
- Attitudes → **situational judgment tests (SJTs)**
- Skills → **OSCEs with AI-related scenarios**, scored with an "AI Clinical
  Decision Conflict Scoring Scale"
- Longitudinal, multi-stage: pre-enrollment → pre-clinical → post-clinical

Integration pathway: embed AI literacy modularly from early undergraduate years;
integrated curriculum covering fundamental principles, advanced large-model
applications (prompt engineering, agent development), and ethics.

### 4.7 McGrath et al. — AI-PACE

- McGrath SP, Kim KK, Johl K, Wang H, Anderson N. *npj Digit Med.* 2026;9(1).
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-02768-2) (PMID 42141085)
- **Method:** integrative review of 23 peer-reviewed articles (2016-2025).
- **PACE = Psychomotor, Affective, Cognitive, Embedded** — a Bloom's
  Taxonomy-grounded structure organizing AI competencies longitudinally across
  UME, GME, and CME.
- **Three structural gaps identified** (these are the failure modes to design
  against):
  1. short-term interventions without reinforcement
  2. procedural-field bias (radiology/pathology overrepresented, generalist
     medicine underserved)
  3. consistent **under-representation of the Affective domain**

### 4.8 Simoni et al. — Principles for medical students' responsible use of generative AI

The only LLM-specific, student-partnered consensus document. Distinct from
competencies — this is conduct policy for students.

- Simoni J, Pereira JL, Aschenbrenner-Noriega I, et al. *BMC Med Ethics.* 2026.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01550-z) (PMID 42399988)
- **Method:** four-round modified Delphi, May 2025-Feb 2026. In-person idea
  generation, two rating rounds (7-point Likert, median/IQR decision rules), then
  external validation. Panel: 13 medical students + 2 professors; final round
  validated by 9 international experts in AI medical education, AI ethics, AI
  technology education, and senior medical educators running AI curricula.
- **Output:** **14 consensus principles** for GenAI use in UME.
- **Why it matters here:** it is co-designed *with* students, and the authors
  explicitly intend it to be adapted to local institutional context. Use it as the
  template for your course's student AI-use policy.

### 4.9 Ma et al. — Promoting AI Competencies for Medical Students (scoping review)

- Ma Y, Song Y, Balch JA, et al. arXiv preprint arXiv:2407.18939, July 2024.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18939 (preprint; not peer-reviewed)
- **Four dimensions of AI literacy:** Foundational, Practical, Experimental,
  Ethical — with learning objectives tailored to **pre-clinical, clinical, and
  clinical research stages**.
- Authors' caveat: guidelines need greater clinical relevance and personalization
  to student interests and career trajectories.

### 4.10 Tolentino et al. — Curriculum frameworks and educational programs (scoping review)

Best available **frequency data** on what existing programs actually teach — this
is what the synthesis below is weighted against.

- Tolentino R, Baradaran A, Gore G, Pluye P, Abbasgholizadeh-Rahimi S.
  *JMIR Med Educ.* 2024. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/54793) (PMID 39023999)
- Covers medical students, residents, and practicing physicians; 21 included
  papers. Only **two** frameworks found (Masters' general framework; Valikodath
  et al. ophthalmology residency framework).

Topic prevalence across programs:

| Topic | % of programs |
|---|---|
| Clinical AI applications | 100% |
| Fundamentals of AI and machine learning | 79% |
| Ethical, legal, and economic considerations | 58% |
| Healthcare data science foundations | 53% |
| Operating AI tools in healthcare settings | 53% |
| Strengths and limitations of AI systems | 47% |
| Impact on clinical reasoning and decision-making | 37% |
| Critical appraisal of AI systems | 37% |
| Patient communication regarding AI | 21% |

**Notable gap:** patient communication about AI appears in only 21% of programs
despite being named in nearly every framework. So does the finding that **no
reviewed program referenced any underlying learning theory or pedagogy**.

## 5. Existing courses and curricula

Published implementation reports remain thin relative to the number of framework
papers — this is the weakest part of the evidence base, and the Hunt et al. review
makes the same point (sources predominantly editorial rather than empirical).

### 5.1 Kim, Kang & Lee — Twelve tips for developing and implementing AI curriculum for UME

**The single most practically useful implementation paper. Read this first.**

- Kim DH, Kang YJ, Lee YM. *Med Educ Online.* 2025;30(1):2585637.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/10872981.2025.2585637) (PMID 41327942)

Guidance in brief: define purpose and scope of AI education within existing
competency frameworks and digital health rather than inventing a parallel
structure; structure content for **progressive deepening** across years;
prioritize key elements rather than covering everything; secure institutional
resources for sustainability; give learners **authentic experiences**; and build
in continuous evaluation and improvement. From the same group as the Korean
Delphi (4.3), so it pairs directly with that competency set.

### 5.2 Thomae, Witt & Barth — ChatGPT integrated into a blended-learning course (University of Zurich)

The best-evaluated LLM-specific teaching intervention published.

- Thomae AV, Witt CM, Barth J. *JMIR Med Educ.* 2024;10:e50545.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/50545) (PMID 39177012)
- **Format:** ChatGPT integrated into 3 teaching units of an existing blended
  learning course. 52 medical students. Mixed methods, evaluated on the
  **Kirkpatrick model** (satisfaction, learning progress, applicability).
- **What they actually did:** students critically reviewed patient-facing
  information written by ChatGPT 3.5 under different prompts, and compared videos
  of patient information for persuasiveness and effect on treatment expectations.
- **Results:** high satisfaction, learning progress, and applicability for the
  ChatGPT units specifically; all three evaluation levels correlated. Higher
  openness to digital innovation predicted higher satisfaction. Students rated AI
  competencies elsewhere in the curriculum as highly important.
- **The transferable finding:** output from a **basic prompt** was rated only
  "moderate" on comprehensibility, patient safety, and correct application of
  taught communication rules; an **extended prompt** substantially improved
  ratings. But the AI-written text still produced the *smallest* increase in
  treatment expectations compared with information delivered by a patient,
  clinician, or expert. That contrast is an excellent teaching exercise — it
  demonstrates both prompt sensitivity and the limits of AI-mediated
  communication in one activity.

### 5.3 Samaranayake et al. — Health informatics and AI curriculum needs assessment (UNT Health Science Center)

- Samaranayake N, Hassan AK, Nelson L, Lieto J. *Med Sci Educ.* 2025;36(1):53-62.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40670-025-02608-0) (PMID 41939041)
- Cross-sectional needs-assessment survey for a **novel longitudinal curriculum**
  designed on the **Master Adaptive Learner model** and experiential learning
  theory.
- **Student-ranked topic interest:** EHRs 88%, evidence-based medicine 85%,
  telemedicine 83%.
- **Preferred modalities:** case studies 70%, simulations 69%.
- Useful as a template for running your own needs assessment before finalizing
  content, and as evidence that students want experiential over didactic delivery.

### 5.4 Li & Qin — Student perception, curriculum recommendations, design suggestions

- Li Q, Qin Y. *BMC Med Educ.* 2023;23(1):852.
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04700-8) (PMID 37946176)
- 1,243 undergraduate and postgraduate students, 13 universities, 33 hospitals;
  54.3% had prior medical AI experience. Analyzed with UTAUT2.
- Intention to use medical AI was positively associated with **performance
  expectancy, habit, hedonic motivation, and trust**. Postgraduates showed higher
  awareness than undergraduates.
- **Design implication:** courses should be easy to learn and engaging (hedonic
  motivation is a real predictor), and should demonstrate concrete performance
  benefit early.

## 6. Synthesis: consensus competency domains

The following domains recur across Russell et al., Hunt et al., Lee et al.,
Singla et al., DECODE, CAIP, AI-PACE, the CGEA educator framework, and the AAMC's
stated goals. They are ordered so that the first six form a defensible required
core and the last three are elective extensions — an ordering supported by the
Korean Delphi's essential/optional split and DECODE's mandatory/discretionary
structure.

### Core (required)

**1. Foundations of AI and data**
What AI and machine learning are; supervised/unsupervised learning; how LLMs
differ from earlier AI; training data, model outputs, and probabilistic
reasoning; terminology; the role of human intervention (RLHF, curation) in LLMs.
*Appears in: every framework reviewed. 79% of existing programs (Tolentino).*

**2. Critical appraisal and evaluation of AI tools and outputs**
Evaluating quality, accuracy, safety, calibration, contextual appropriateness,
and generalizability; understanding validation, dataset shift, and performance
degradation across populations; recognizing hallucination and fabricated
citations; knowing when AI is not appropriate for a task.
*Named as one of the top three recurring emphases in Hunt et al. Russell domain 4;
Hunt domain 5; CGEA domain C.*

**3. Bias, equity, and the social implications of AI**
Sources of data and output bias; how personal and structural bias enters inputs
and outputs; black-box models and interpretability; differential performance
across demographic groups; how social, economic, and political systems shape AI
tools and their justice/equity consequences.
*Russell domain 2; Hunt domain 1; CAIP's two highest-ranked bias competencies
(CAIP4, CAIP12).*

**4. Ethics, law, regulation, and professionalism**
Data privacy (HIPAA/FERPA); informed consent and transparency with patients;
liability and accountability; FDA/regulatory pathways for AI-enabled devices;
attribution and academic integrity in AI-assisted work; automation bias and
deliberate skill preservation; professional responsibility for AI-influenced
decisions.
*The most-emphasized area across the entire corpus (Hunt et al.: "ethicolegal
oversight"). Ethical use is also the single most common required topic in US
medical schools per AAMC survey data. Hunt domains 1-3; Russell domain 2; Lee
domain 3; CGEA domain D.*

**5. Clinical application and practical use of AI**
Using AI tools in real clinical workflows; clinical decision support; ambient
documentation; diagnostic and imaging AI; prompt construction and iteration for
LLMs; interpreting AI output within clinical reasoning; maintaining
physician-led decision-making with AI as adjunct.
*Present in 100% of existing programs (Tolentino). Russell domain 3; Hunt domain
4; Lee domain 4; CAIP16. Note the LCME's proposed Element 7.2 requires
**experiential** learning here.*

**6. Communication with patients about AI**
Explaining to patients when and how AI is involved in their care; disclosure and
consent conversations; addressing patient concerns and misconceptions;
recognizing that AI-generated patient information is systematically less
persuasive and requires clinician mediation.
*Named in Russell (domain 3), Singla (communication), Hunt (professionalism), and
the AAMC goals — but taught in only 21% of existing programs. **This is the
largest gap between consensus and practice, and the clearest differentiator for a
new curriculum.***

### Extension (elective or advanced track)

**7. Workflow, systems, and implementation**
How AI changes teams, roles, responsibilities, and workflows; human factors and
alert fatigue; implementation science; local governance and procurement;
monitoring deployed tools.
*Russell domain 5; DECODE health information systems; CAIP health data governance.
Under-taught relative to its importance.*

**8. Health data science**
Data provenance, quality, structure, and governance; basic statistics for model
evaluation (sensitivity/specificity, AUC, calibration, NNT-equivalents for AI);
working with EHR data; collaborating effectively with data scientists.
*Lee domain 5 — rated **optional** by a higher proportion of experts. DECODE
domain 4. 53% of existing programs. Reasonable as a required-light / elective-deep
split.*

**9. Research, innovation, and scholarship with AI**
Evaluating AI research literature and reporting standards; using AI tools in
literature search, synthesis, and writing with proper attribution; IRB
considerations for AI-generated data; participating in AI development and
validation studies.
*Lee domain 6 — also rated **optional** more often. Hunt domain 6; CGEA domain G.*

### Cross-cutting: professional identity and adaptability

Not a content domain but a stance that every framework asserts and most curricula
omit — AI-PACE identifies the **Affective domain as consistently
under-represented**, and the Macy recommendations call for "reevaluating
foundational physician competencies for the AI era" and "facilitating personal and
organizational adaptability." Concretely: self-assessment of AI knowledge gaps,
building a personal AI learning plan, reflective practice, comfort with rapid
change, and a considered position on what parts of clinical judgment should not be
delegated. Russell's domain 6 (practice-based learning and improvement) and CGEA's
domain H are the closest published anchors.

## 7. Design recommendations

1. **Build on the Hunt et al. seven-domain taxonomy** as the backbone — it is the
   most current, most comprehensive, and explicitly designed as "a structured
   inventory to inform curriculum planning." Map your objectives to Russell et al.
   as well, since that is what US reviewers will recognize.

2. **Adopt DECODE's mandatory/discretionary split** for learning outcomes. It is
   the cleanest published solution to the curriculum-space problem.

3. **Make communication with patients about AI a signature element.** Every
   framework names it; almost nobody teaches it. High impact, low cost, and it
   fits existing OSCE infrastructure.

4. **Design for experiential learning, not lectures.** The proposed LCME element
   requires it, students prefer it (UNT: case studies 70%, simulations 69%), and
   AI-PACE identifies short non-reinforced interventions as a primary failure
   mode. Macy's "AI playgrounds" recommendation points the same direction.

5. **Plan assessment up front using the CAIP model** — standardized testing for
   knowledge, situational judgment tests for attitudes, AI-scenario OSCEs for
   skills. Reference AMEE Guide No. 178 for assessment design.

6. **Structure longitudinally with progressive deepening**, not as a one-off
   block. AI-PACE, CAIP, and Kim et al.'s twelve tips all converge on this.

7. **Name a learning theory.** Tolentino et al. found that *no* reviewed program
   referenced one. Master Adaptive Learner (as UNT used) or experiential learning
   are defensible choices and make the work publishable.

8. **Run faculty development in parallel** using the CGEA educator competencies
   (4.3, section 1.3). AAMC Principle 4 makes this explicit, and it is the most
   common practical failure point.

9. **Write the student GenAI use policy separately** from the competencies, using
   Simoni et al.'s 14 principles as a template — ideally co-designed with students,
   as that study was.

10. **Build for remapping.** The AAMC competencies land in fall 2026. Keep your
    objectives modular and tagged so a crosswalk is a mapping exercise, not a
    rewrite.
